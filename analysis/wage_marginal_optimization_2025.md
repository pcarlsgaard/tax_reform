# Federal marginal-wedge optimization with a 35% top rate

**Updated interpretation (September 2026):** This optimization includes a wage-proportional *employer pension contribution* at the margin, then taxes it as cash under the reform. It does not match the website household wage-only chart. On the website's cash-wage-plus-employer-FICA basis, the starting 25%/35% schedule lowers modeled federal marginal wedges for 59.1% of workers; the earlier 84.1% higher figure below applies only to this broader compensation sensitivity. See the [audited comparison and 30% intermediate bracket](wage_tax_credit_diagnostic_2025.md). The five-bin revenue optimization still protects bin averages rather than maximizing the number of workers with lower rates.

This is a reproducible **exploration, not an all-in federal tax-and-benefit score**. It starts from the user's X-tax with a 35% business and top wage rate, full cash-out of employer compensation, a $7,200 refundable child credit, a $3,000 adult/$1,500 child insurance credit, and replacement of the seven listed social programs. The deficit comparison also keeps the **$16,200 child credit** in the illustrative family safeguard. The child and insurance credits are held fixed as wage brackets and the earned adult credit vary. The existing [Social spending view](../src/views/Transfers.tsx) supplies the seven-program spending ledger, the safeguard credit and illustrative household earnings traces, including a simplified [SNAP rule](../src/model/transfers.ts). The national optimizer takes its static fiscal reference from that scenario, but cannot directly extrapolate its example households into representative program receipt.

## What can be protected

The prior federal *tax-only* calculation compared each worker's +$1,000 wage increment after federal income tax, EITC/CTC, and **both** employee and employer FICA, on a common employer-cost denominator. Health coverage is fixed at the margin and pension contributions grow with pay. This calculation excludes state and local taxes as requested. Its simplified income-tax calculator also groups heads of household with single filers, so the tax-only baseline requires validation against a fuller federal calculator.

For a broader **federal resource wedge**, add the lost value of federal benefits that the reform replaces: SNAP, housing assistance, TANF, child nutrition, and the current ACA premium tax credit. [CBO defines](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/43709) a resource-based marginal wedge as the share of extra earnings lost to taxes or reduced benefits. Retained Medicaid affects both sides, provided its eligibility is unchanged. The [Census CPS ASEC](https://cdn.www.census.gov/topics/income-poverty/income/guidance/tax-model.html) identifies some benefit recipients but does not establish their precise earnings-dependent withdrawal or timing. In particular, the estimates below vary **SNAP and housing** withdrawal while leaving TANF, child nutrition, ACA subsidy withdrawal, benefit cliffs and AMT/NIIT effects unmodeled. They should not be presented as complete current-law marginal rates.

In the **current simplified benchmark**, an unconditional promise that **no worker** sees a higher marginal rate is incompatible with a uniform **35% effective rate above $300,000 per filing adult**. About **0.17% of workers** are in that top bracket and have a modeled current federal tax-only wedge below 35%, even before considering the other omitted rules. With the original earned-credit design, at least **1.23% of workers** have an unavoidable increase even if all lower wage rates are reduced to zero. Household-specific offsets or changes to the top effective rate would be necessary for a literal individual guarantee in this benchmark. These percentages must be rechecked with the complete current-law wedge.

The solvable constraint here is narrower: **the earnings-weighted average marginal rate in each of five individual cash-wage bins may not rise**. Individual workers within each bin can still face an increase. The optimizer maximizes retained **annual static wage revenue** among monotone rates starting at $0/$20,000/$75,000/$150,000/$300,000 **per filing adult**, with the final rate fixed at 35%. It evaluates earned adult credits of $2,000–$5,000 and 10%–20% phase-in rates on a declared 20-point grid. The search is exact within each fixed credit combination and the specified five-bracket structure; it does not search all possible policy designs.

The five bracket entries cover tax-unit compensation **$0–20k**, **$20–75k**, **$75–150k**, **$150–300k**, and **above $300k**, each threshold multiplied by the number of filing adults. The original wage schedule is **25% through $75k per filing adult, then 35%**; its annual static deficit reduction with the $16,200 child credit is **+$190.7B**, and **84.1%** of workers have a higher modeled federal tax-only marginal rate. These results are static comparisons; they do not rerun the capital or labor equilibrium.

The withdrawal sensitivities use **observed SPM receipt**, then assume that another $1,000 in annual gross wages reduces SNAP by up to **24%** of those wages and housing assistance by up to **30%**, subject to each household's observed annual subsidy. Housing benefits are valued at **75%** of government cost. The middle row halves both assumed effects. The 24% SNAP rate follows from the [usual 20% earned-income deduction and 30% allotment reduction on net income](https://www.fna.usda.gov/snap/recipient/eligibility); [30% of *adjusted* income](https://www.hud.gov/helping-americans/public-housing-energy-branch-util) is a common assisted-housing tenant contribution. Real eligibility, deductions, reporting intervals, shelter interactions and alternative rules cause substantial deviations. **The SPM housing field covers more than the tenant-based vouchers proposed for repeal**; using all of its positive observations overstates the number of households whose housing withdrawal disappears. The full-withdrawal row is an **illustrative high-effect scenario**, not a validated estimate of recipients' true marginal losses. Because the assumed withdrawal materially changes the revenue conclusion, there is no defensible single "all-in" revenue number yet.

In the tax-only run, expanding the earned adult credit from $2,000 to $3,000 and its phase-in from 10% to 12% costs an additional **$173.7B/year** before increased wage revenue from the reoptimized bracket rates. Refundable credits given as flat amounts, including the child credit, do not lower marginal rates outside their phase-in or phaseout regions. The optimum can therefore raise some statutory bracket rates while meeting the five *average* constraints.

## Reproduction and next validation

With NumPy and SciPy installed, run `python3 scripts/optimize_wage_marginals.py --archive /path/to/asecpub25csv.zip`. The archive SHA-256 is `318845a2b5e0034eb2973898de1738f4df0025727de38499e7669cb9c0deef0b`. The [aggregate JSON](wage_marginal_optimization_2025.json) records the five bin averages, schedule parameters, fiscal changes and limitations; no respondent-level data are saved.

To turn this into the requested **individual-level federal guarantee**, link each tax unit to actual current SNAP/housing/ACA receipt and recompute benefit awards after the wage change; upgrade the current-law tax calculation for head-of-household status, AMT, NIIT and the other missing provisions, preferably with an independent federal tax simulator. Then solve the individual constraints and report an infeasibility certificate with the fixed 35% top rate, or specify the narrowly targeted marginal offsets that make a guarantee feasible. Verify the static revenue independently and rerun the labor response under the finalized schedule. Current CPS public data alone cannot establish all benefit cliffs or receipt accurately enough to certify that guarantee.
